1. Ethnography

Definition: studying cultures and communities by immersing oneself in participants' natural environment to understand their behaviours, beliefs and practices. Uses in-depth observation, interviews and analysis to give a rich, detailed picture from the perspective of those studied.

Critical case — Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum (William Foote Whyte, 1943)
  • Overt participant observation — identity not hidden
  • Key informant strategy — local leader "Doc" for access and rapport
  • Long-term immersion — roughly four years; lived with an Italian family; learned Italian (book p. 245)
  • Criticisms: (1) direct engagement in illegal activities — vote fraud, racketeering; (2) Doc's influence — rivalry with Chick, his local opponent; (3) anonymity issues

2. Overt vs covert ethnography

Overt (overt participation)Covert (covert / complete participation)
MeaningResearcher openly declares role and intentionsResearcher does not reveal identity as a researcher
ProsMore ethical; easier communication; participants can withhold info; researcher can refuse unethical acts (p. 243)More access because of hidden identity; reduces reactive effect
ConsMay impact participant behaviour (reactive effect)Less ethical; stressful for researcher; risk of "going native"
ExamplesWhyte (1943); VenkateshHumphreys (1970) "watch queen"; Alfred (1976) Satanists; Goffman (1961) hospital assistant

3. Phenomenology

  • Explores how individuals experience and make sense of phenomena — subjective meanings of lived experience
  • Not objective explanations or cause-and-effect relationships
  • Captures "what it is like" to experience something; richness and depth of individual perspectives
  • Methods: interviews · observations · personal narratives

4. Interview types

TypeKey features
StructuredFixed set of predetermined questions, specific order, limited flexibility
Semi-structuredOpen-ended core questions; can explore unexpected topics with follow-up probes while staying focused
UnstructuredNo fixed questions or order; broad opening statement; respondent guides the organic flow

Intensive interviewing (book, p. 237): open-ended, relatively unstructured questioning seeking in-depth information on the interviewee's feelings, experiences and/or perceptions. Class exercise: write 10–12 semi-structured questions for your research report.

5. Case study

In-depth qualitative approach examining a specific real-world phenomenon within its context; a single case or small number of cases; multiple sources of evidence.

Limitations (4): limited generalizability · potential for researcher bias · time-consuming and resource-intensive · difficulty in replication.

6. Fundamentals of qualitative methods (pp. 236–238)

Three designs: participant observation, intensive interviewing, focus groups. Distinctive features:

  • Collection primarily of qualitative data
  • Exploratory research questions + inductive reasoning
  • Focus on previously unstudied processes and unanticipated phenomena
  • Orientation to social context (Venkatesh's Blackstone)
  • Focus on human subjectivity — meanings people give to their lives
  • Focus on events leading up to a particular event/outcome
  • Reflexive research design — design develops as research progresses (Maxwell, 1996)
  • Sensitivity to the subjective role of the researcher — Exhibit 9.1 (Miller & Crabtree, 1999): gathering and analysis proceed together

Field research (p. 238): natural social processes studied as they happen, left relatively undisturbed. Origins: anthropologists Franz Boas (Native American villages) and Bronislaw Malinowski (New Guinea); Chicago School treated the city as a social science laboratory.

7. Case study: Life in a Gang (Decker & Van Winkle, 1996)

  • Gang = "age-graded peer group that exhibits some permanence, engages in criminal activity, and has some symbolic representation of membership"
  • Membership by self-identification — "Are you claiming…?"
  • Field ethnographer's direct observation + intensive interviews with open-ended questions and probes; inductive analysis using gang members' own categories
  • Thick description (Geertz, 1973): rich description of what it is like from the participants' standpoint

8. Participant observation & roles (pp. 240–244)

Participant observation: sustained, intensive relationship with people while they go about their normal activities. Observational continuum (Exhibit 9.2):

RoleMeaningExample
Complete observerDoes not participate; publicly defined as researcherSusan Miller (1999) — community policing, shadowing officers
Participant and observerDiscloses role but participatesWhyte (1943); Venkatesh (2000) — "hang out with them"
Covert participantHides role; acts like a member (complete participation)Humphreys (1970); Alfred (1976); Goffman (1961)

Reactive effect: changes in behaviour due to being observed. Miller reduced it by becoming a familiar face over 18 months.

Covert participants' problems: cannot openly take notes · cannot ask suspicious questions · role hard to play (Alfred seen as "strange") · must keep up the act → psychological strain · risk of going native (adopting participants' goals and abandoning research goals).

Ethics: Erikson (1967) — covert research is unethical, only for public settings · Punch (1994) — total ban would "kill many a project stone dead" · Pruitt (2008) — escorts replied 15% to a sociologist vs 60% to a "client" · ASA: deception only if not harmful, justified, no feasible alternative, minimal risk, IRB approval.

9. Entering the field & relationships (pp. 245–247)

  • Background work; learn participants' dress, activities, language (Whyte learned Italian)
  • Develop trust with at least one informant — Miller via the police chief and two officer friends
  • Confidentiality = cornerstone of trust (Decker & Van Winkle refused to share others' answers → seen as "solid"); Jody Miller (2000) started with innocuous questions
  • Advice (Whyte; Wolcott): honest explanation · keep key individuals' support · don't question too aggressively · don't fake social similarity · avoid money/gifts but respect reciprocity · expect tension between groups

10. Sampling people and events (p. 247)

  • One setting = intensive portrait but weak generalization; several settings strengthen conclusions (King, Keohane & Verba, 1994)
  • Purposive sampling, often with a snowball element — Decker & Van Winkle: 500+ contacts → final sample of 99
  • Theoretical sampling (Glaser & Strauss, 1967): settings/individuals drawn sequentially as earlier observations show they are influential — used to include female, older and non-Black gang members
