## 1. Introduction

Lawyers are officers of the court. Professional rules require them to act independently, honestly and in their client's lawful interest. Freidson (2001) describes professionalism as a "third logic", distinct from both the market and bureaucracy, that depends on practitioners being free to exercise judgement according to professional values. That freedom is not guaranteed. In Bangladesh, the legal profession works within an environment shaped by intense political competition, partisan Bar associations, a strongly hierarchical apprenticeship culture and court offices where discretion over files and dates rests with staff. Lipsky (1980) would call these staff "street-level bureaucrats".

Public debate on justice in Bangladesh tends to focus on backlog and delay. Far less attention is paid to the pressures on lawyers themselves: being asked to "manage" a case, being warned not to "push too hard", depending on a senior for income, or being treated differently because of one's gender. These pressures directly affect whether lawyers *can* pursue justice, and at what personal cost.

This report addresses the research question: ***What challenges do lawyers face as they strive to pursue justice within legal institutions?*** It focuses on the **professional and ethical dimension** of that question: external pressure and integrity dilemmas, the hierarchy and gender dynamics of the Bar, and their effects on lawyers' wellbeing. Section 2 describes the methodology. Section 3 presents the thematic findings with quotations and a coding paradigm model. Section 4 discusses limitations, and Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Research design

The study followed a **qualitative, interpretivist approach**, appropriate for understanding how people make sense of sensitive experiences in their own terms (Creswell & Poth, 2018). **Semi-structured in-depth interviews** were used. They provide comparable data across respondents while leaving room to probe difficult topics gently and to follow respondents' own priorities (Kvale & Brinkmann, 2009).

### 2.2 Sampling and participants

Respondents were selected from among batchmates, as directed for this assignment. **Purposive sampling** identified ten batchmates who are practising or apprenticing as lawyers, varying in seniority (1 to 12 years), gender (three women) and forum (district courts, tribunals, the labour court and the High Court Division). This variation was important because pressure and hierarchy are experienced differently by juniors and seniors and by men and women. Respondents are referred to as R1–R10 (Table 1; Figure 1).

### 2.3 Data collection

The interview guide contained **twelve open-ended questions** with probes (Appendix A). Questions 6, 8 and 9 were central to this report: they asked about influence and informal payments, professional relationships and hierarchy, and personal impact. Because these topics are sensitive, the guide placed them in the middle of the interview, after rapport had been established, and phrased them in the third person ("experienced or *witnessed*") to reduce discomfort. Interviews were held between [dates] by [mode], lasted roughly [duration] minutes, and were conducted in Bangla and English. They were recorded or noted with consent, then transcribed and translated.

### 2.4 Ethical considerations

Given the sensitivity of questions on political pressure and informal payments, particular care was taken. Respondents gave informed verbal consent, were reminded that they could decline any question, and were assured that no names, cases, courts or individuals would be identifiable in the report. Identifying details were removed at transcription, and data were stored securely for use in this assignment only.

### 2.5 Data analysis

**Thematic analysis** following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six phases was used (Table 2). Line-by-line open coding was followed by **axial coding** using Strauss and Corbin's (1998) **coding paradigm**, which connects categories by identifying the causal conditions, context, intervening conditions, action strategies and consequences surrounding a central phenomenon.

### 2.6 Trustworthiness and reflexivity

A peer reviewed the coding framework, and two respondents were invited to comment on a summary of the findings. An audit trail of coding decisions was kept (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). As a batchmate, I benefited from trust, which was essential for respondents to speak about pressure and payments at all. I was also aware that friendship might lead respondents to soften criticism of colleagues, and that my own views on Bar politics could colour my interpretation. I therefore relied on respondents' own words wherever possible.

### 3.7 Coding paradigm model

Figure 5 integrates the findings using Strauss and Corbin's (1998) coding paradigm. The **central phenomenon** is that lawyers' *professional autonomy and integrity are constrained by pressure*. The **causal conditions** are political influence, pressure from influential parties and a culture of informal payments. These operate in a **context** of partisan Bar politics, a guru-shishya hierarchy and a male-dominated professional culture. **Intervening conditions**, namely juniors' income insecurity, reliance on a senior's name and weak accountability mechanisms, determine how strongly each lawyer feels these pressures. Lawyers respond with **strategies** of refusing to "manage" cases, documenting everything in writing, building peer networks and seeking many mentors rather than one. The **consequences** include ethical dilemmas and lost clients, fear, stress and burnout, attrition among juniors and women, and a clear demand for an independent judicial secretariat and transparent processes.

## 4. Research limitations

The study has important limitations. First, it is based on **ten batchmates** selected purposively and for convenience, and most practise in the Dhaka region, so the findings cannot be generalised to all lawyers in Bangladesh. Second, the topics are **highly sensitive**. Respondents may have under-reported their own involvement in informal payments or political networks, and some spoke in general rather than personal terms. My **insider relationship** with respondents helped them speak openly, but it may also have encouraged socially desirable answers. Third, **timing** was difficult: interviews had to fit around court schedules, and some were shorter or conducted by phone, limiting deeper probing on sensitive points. Fourth, **translation** from Bangla may have lost some nuance and tone. Fifth, analysis was carried out by a **single coder**. A peer review of the coding framework reduced, but did not remove, the risk of interpretive bias.

## 5. Conclusion

This report examined the professional and ethical challenges lawyers face as they try to pursue justice within legal institutions. The interviews show that, alongside delay and poor infrastructure, lawyers have to navigate a **web of pressure**: political and economic influence that is rarely provable but widely felt, a normalised culture of informal payments that forces daily ethical choices, a steep and partly politicised hierarchy within the Bar, and gendered treatment that pushes women to prove themselves twice. These pressures carry real personal costs, including moral fatigue, fear, financial insecurity and, for some juniors and women, departure from the profession. Lawyers are not simply victims of these conditions. They refuse to "manage" cases, document their work, build peer networks and speak openly about reform. Their accounts suggest that protecting lawyers' professional autonomy, through an independent judicial secretariat, transparent listing, accountable investigation and fair conditions for junior and female lawyers, is itself a precondition for justice.
