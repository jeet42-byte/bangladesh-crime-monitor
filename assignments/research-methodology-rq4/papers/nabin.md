## 1. Introduction

Bangladesh inherited a common-law legal system whose core procedural statutes, the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898 and the Evidence Act 1872, were drafted for a colonial administration and a far smaller population. The judiciary was formally separated from the executive in 2007, following the Appellate Division's directions in *Secretary, Ministry of Finance v. Masdar Hossain* (2000). Separation has not, however, solved the problem that dominates public discussion of the courts: delay. Supreme Court figures widely reported in the national press put the number of pending cases above four million, handled by roughly two thousand subordinate-court judges for a population of more than 170 million.

Most debate on this backlog is statistical. Far less is known about how delay is *experienced* by the professionals who must work inside it. Lawyers sit at the junction between citizens and institutions: they file the papers, attend the dates, absorb the clients' frustration and, as this study shows, sometimes add to the delay themselves. Their accounts show how "justice delayed" happens in daily practice.

This report addresses the assigned research question: ***What challenges do lawyers face as they strive to pursue justice within legal institutions?*** It focuses on the **institutional and procedural** side of that question: delay, administrative bottlenecks and weak evidence, and the strategies lawyers use to work around them. Section 2 explains how the research was carried out. Section 3 presents the findings of a thematic analysis, supported by respondents' own words and by a coding paradigm model. Section 4 discusses limitations, and Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Research design

The study used a **qualitative, interpretivist design**. The aim was not to measure how often delay occurs but to understand what it means to lawyers and how they respond to it, which calls for rich, first-person accounts (Creswell & Poth, 2018). **Semi-structured in-depth interviews** were chosen because they combine a consistent set of core questions with the flexibility to follow up unexpected points through probes (Kvale & Brinkmann, 2009).

### 2.2 Sampling and participants

Following the course instruction, respondents were recruited from among batchmates. **Purposive sampling** was used to select ten batchmates who are currently practising or apprenticing as lawyers. Variation was sought in years of experience, gender and forum of practice, from district courts and specialised tribunals to the High Court Division. Ten interviews is consistent with evidence that the main themes in a fairly homogeneous group tend to emerge within the first dozen interviews (Guest, Bunce & Johnson, 2006). ### 2.3 Data collection

An interview guide of **twelve open-ended questions** with follow-up probes was developed from the research question (Appendix A). The questions moved from the general (the respondent's journey into law and their idea of justice) to specific institutional experiences: delay, court administration, evidence, pressure, client circumstances, professional relationships and personal impact. They closed with coping strategies, digital reforms and one desired change. Interviews were conducted in [mode: face-to-face / phone / online] between [dates], lasted approximately [duration] minutes and were held in Bangla, English or a mix, as the respondent preferred. With consent, interviews were audio-recorded or noted in detail, then transcribed and translated into English, keeping the respondents' original terms (e.g., *peshkar*, *kharcha*, *dalal*).

### 2.4 Ethical considerations

Each respondent was told the purpose of the study, that participation was voluntary, and that they could skip any question or withdraw at any time. Verbal informed consent was obtained before each interview. Because some questions touched on sensitive matters such as informal payments and political pressure, no names, case numbers or identifying details are reported, and recordings and notes were stored securely and used only for this assignment.

### 2.5 Data analysis

Transcripts were analysed using **Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase thematic analysis**, combined with Strauss and Corbin's (1998) **coding paradigm** at the axial-coding stage. Coding was done manually in a spreadsheet: each meaningful segment of an answer was assigned one or more codes, and the codes were then grouped into themes. Table 2 shows how each phase was applied.

### 2.6 Trustworthiness and reflexivity

Credibility was strengthened by briefly checking interpretations with two respondents (member checking) and discussing the emerging codes with a classmate (peer debriefing). An audit trail of transcripts, code lists and the framework matrix was kept (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). As a practising lawyer and a batchmate of the respondents, I held an **insider position**. This made access and rapport easier, but it also risked my assuming shared meanings, so I deliberately probed terms that "everyone knows", such as *time petition*.

### 3.5 Coding paradigm model

To show how the themes relate to one another, the axial codes were organised using Strauss and Corbin's (1998) coding paradigm (Figure 5). The **central phenomenon** is protracted, procedurally obstructed litigation, which can be summarised as "justice by date". Its **causal conditions** are judicial under-capacity, an entrenched adjournment culture and weak, late evidence. It occurs in a **context** of overloaded cause lists, paper-based record rooms and multiple cases arising from a single dispute. **Intervening conditions** such as "speed money", dependency on seniors and uneven digitisation make the problem worse or, occasionally, ease it. Lawyers respond with **strategies** of persistence, precise drafting, mediation and informal digital workarounds. The **consequences** are clients who lose faith, prolonged pre-trial detention, frustrated and exhausted lawyers, and a clear professional demand for case-management reform.

### 3.6 Connections with the remaining themes

Although they are not the focus of this report, the other three themes show how institutional delay spreads outward. Delay multiplies litigation costs for poor clients (T5). It creates space for political and social pressure to influence outcomes (T4). It also weighs heavily on junior lawyers, whose income depends on cases that do not conclude (T6). As R6 summarised, "the cost of coming to court fifty times" is itself a barrier to justice.

## 4. Research limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the **sample was small and drawn from batchmates** through convenience and purposive selection, and most respondents practise in and around Dhaka. The findings therefore reflect the experiences of a particular group of relatively young, urban lawyers and cannot be generalised to the profession as a whole. Second, my **insider position** as a lawyer and classmate helped rapport but may have encouraged respondents to give answers they expected I would agree with, or to under-report sensitive issues such as informal payments. Third, **scheduling interviews with practising lawyers** was difficult. Court hours left limited time, so some interviews were shorter than planned or conducted by phone, which reduced the depth of probing. Fourth, **translation** from Bangla to English may have lost some nuance, although key local terms were kept. Finally, coding was carried out by a **single researcher**. Peer debriefing and an audit trail reduced, but did not eliminate, the risk of subjective interpretation.

## 5. Conclusion

This study asked what challenges lawyers face in pursuing justice within legal institutions, and it examined the institutional and procedural side of that question. The interviews show that delay is the defining experience of legal practice for these respondents. It is built from repeated adjournments, overloaded cause lists and too few judges, and it is reinforced by paper-based administration, informal payments to staff, and investigations and evidence that arrive late or not at all. Lawyers are not passive in the face of these problems. They chase files, draft for short hearings, push for mediation and build their own digital workarounds, and some openly admit that their own time petitions are part of the problem. The coding paradigm model shows a self-reinforcing cycle: weak capacity produces delay, delay creates space for informal practices, and those practices produce further delay. Respondents' proposals point to where the cycle could be broken: more judges and courts, enforceable timelines for each stage of a case, digitised and integrated records, and accountable investigation. Without such reforms, the respondents suggest, the courts will continue to deliver "justice by date" rather than justice on the merits.
