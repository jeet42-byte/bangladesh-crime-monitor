## 1. Introduction

Access to justice is usually measured from the supply side: the number of courts, judges and pending cases. Cappelletti and Garth (1978) argued that formal rights mean little unless ordinary people can actually use the institutions that enforce them. Galanter (1974) showed that legal systems tend to favour "repeat players" with resources over "one-shotters" who come to court once, often poor and inexperienced. Both insights fit Bangladesh closely. The state has created a statutory legal-aid system under the Legal Aid Act 2000, run through the National Legal Aid Services Organisation, district legal-aid offices and a national helpline (16430). Yet the people most in need of justice, such as day labourers, garment workers, rural landholders and women facing violence or abandonment, still face formidable barriers before they ever reach a judge.

Lawyers see these barriers at close range. They meet clients after a tout has taken their money, watch survivors abandon cases under pressure, and calculate whether a worker can afford to lose another day's wage for another court date. Their accounts therefore offer a practical view of how the justice system looks *from the client's side of the counter*.

This report responds to the research question: ***What challenges do lawyers face as they strive to pursue justice within legal institutions?*** It focuses on the challenge lawyers repeatedly described as the most frustrating: **pursuing justice for clients who cannot easily afford, understand or safely use the legal system**. Section 2 sets out the methodology. Section 3 presents themes from the interviews, supported by quotations and a coding paradigm model. Section 4 notes limitations, and Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Research design

A **qualitative design** was adopted because the study is concerned with meanings, experiences and perceptions rather than frequencies (Creswell & Poth, 2018). The data were gathered through **semi-structured in-depth interviews**. This format keeps a common set of questions across respondents but allows the interviewer to probe individual stories in depth, which is essential when the subject matter involves clients' hardship (Kvale & Brinkmann, 2009).

### 2.2 Sampling and participants

As instructed, respondents were drawn from batchmates. Using **purposive sampling**, ten batchmates who are practising or apprenticing lawyers were selected, with deliberate variation across practice areas whose clients face different kinds of barriers: criminal, family, labour, land and legal-aid work. Three of the ten respondents are women, which allowed gendered experiences to be explored. All respondents are identified by codes (R1–R10). Their profile is given in Table 1 and Figure 1.

### 2.3 Data collection

A guide of **twelve open-ended questions**, each with optional probes, was used (Appendix A). Questions 2, 5, 7 and 10 were especially important for this report because they asked about obstacles in typical cases, evidence, clients' circumstances and legal aid, and coping strategies. Interviews took place between [dates] in [mode], lasted around [duration] minutes, and were conducted in Bangla and/or English. Responses were recorded with permission, or written down in detail where recording was declined, and later transcribed and translated.

### 2.4 Ethical considerations

Informed verbal consent was obtained after explaining the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of participation and the right to withdraw. Respondents were asked not to name clients. Any case details that could identify a client were removed during transcription. Respondents' identities are known only to the researcher.

### 2.5 Data analysis

The transcripts were analysed through **thematic analysis** following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six phases (Table 2). Initial open codes were generated line by line. The codes were then related to one another through **axial coding** using Strauss and Corbin's (1998) coding paradigm, which asks what causes a phenomenon, in what context it occurs, what conditions intervene, how actors respond and with what consequences.

### 2.6 Trustworthiness

To enhance credibility, emerging themes were discussed with a peer, and a summary was shared with two respondents for comment. A record of coding decisions was maintained (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Being a batchmate of the respondents created trust, but I remained conscious that shared assumptions about "how courts work" could lead me to take answers at face value, so I used probes to ask for concrete examples.

### 3.7 Coding paradigm model

Figure 5 integrates these findings using Strauss and Corbin's (1998) coding paradigm. The **central phenomenon** is that justice is *priced and distanced out of ordinary clients' reach*. Its **causal conditions** are litigation cost and lost wages, low legal literacy and distance from courts. These operate in a **context** where delay multiplies costs, touts exploit clients on court premises and gendered social norms restrict women. **Intervening conditions**, namely weak awareness and low pay for legal aid, witness intimidation and poor investigation of poor clients' cases, deepen the exclusion. Lawyers respond with **strategies** of honest explanation, reconciliation and conciliation, referral to NGOs and crisis centres, and use of the legal-aid hotline. The **consequences** include abandoned cases, compromise under pressure, eroded public trust and calls for stronger legal aid and victim protection.

## 4. Research limitations

This study has several limitations. The **sample of ten batchmates** is small and was selected for convenience and relevance, and most respondents practise in Dhaka and nearby districts. The findings therefore illuminate but cannot represent the experiences of lawyers across Bangladesh, particularly in remote areas where barriers may be even greater. Second, the study relies on **lawyers' accounts of their clients**. Clients themselves were not interviewed, so their perspectives are filtered through their lawyers' interpretations. Third, **arranging interviews** around court schedules was difficult, and some interviews were shorter or conducted remotely, which limited follow-up. Fourth, sensitive topics such as intimidation and informal payments may have been **under-reported**, both out of caution and because of my insider relationship with respondents. Fifth, **translation** between Bangla and English may have flattened some expressions. Finally, a **single coder** analysed the data. Peer debriefing helped, but interpretation inevitably reflects the researcher's perspective.

## 5. Conclusion

This study explored the challenges lawyers face in pursuing justice, with a focus on the barriers that stand between their clients and the courts. The interviews suggest that, for many Bangladeshis, the obstacle is not the absence of rights but the **cost, complexity and risk of claiming them**. Repeated court dates turn modest fees into crippling expense. Low legal literacy exposes clients to touts. Women face social and physical barriers at every stage. Weak investigation and witness intimidation mean that poor and vulnerable clients often cannot prove even strong cases. Delay ties these barriers together, multiplying each one. Lawyers respond creatively by explaining the process honestly, turning to mediation and conciliation, and connecting clients with NGOs, crisis centres and the legal-aid hotline. They are clear, however, that individual effort cannot substitute for institutional change. Their recommendations are to pay and evaluate legal-aid lawyers properly, enact victim and witness protection, enforce decrees quickly and bring services closer to clients. These point to a practical reform agenda for making justice reachable rather than merely available.
