# From mobile wallet to scam compound: a quantitative study of cyber-enabled investment fraud in Bangladesh

**PhD research proposal (draft 2, quantitative design, about 2 pages)** · Criminology / Cybercrime · Proposed start: early 2028

## 1. Background
Cyber-enabled investment fraud (often called "pig-butchering") is one of the world's fastest-growing criminal economies. It is run from scam compounds in Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos and laundered through mobile wallets, banks and crypto-assets (UNODC 2023; FATF 2026).

Bangladesh sits at three points in this economy:
- Its citizens are **victims**, approached via Facebook, WhatsApp and Telegram.
- Its mobile financial services (MFS) and bank accounts serve as **mule channels**.
- Its young job-seekers are **lured into compounds** by fake job offers.

A scoping review of Bangladeshi cybercrime research (2,207 records; 82 full-text studies charted) found that:
- Most studies are legal-doctrinal or descriptive.
- Prevalence figures come from small convenience samples.
- Only **one study** applies criminological theory to official data: a crime-script analysis of 94 police complaints by police and Bangladesh Bank officers (Tuhin et al., 2025).
- No study measures fraud victimisation with a large sample, tests why victims fall for or report scams, or measures how scams are marketed online.

## 2. Aim and research questions
**Aim:** to measure and explain investment-fraud victimisation, scam marketing and reporting in Bangladesh, using three quantitative studies.

- **RQ1 (risk):** How common is investment and MFS fraud victimisation, and do lifestyle-routine activities (online exposure, investment-group membership, guardianship, digital literacy) predict it?
- **RQ2 (persuasion):** Which features of a scam message (authority cues, promised returns, platform, payment rail) increase people's willingness to transfer money? This is tested experimentally.
- **RQ3 (reporting):** What predicts reporting to police, MFS providers or banks, and does expected police treatment (procedural justice) change reporting intentions? This is tested experimentally.
- **RQ4 (market):** How are investment scams and overseas "job" lures marketed on public social-media channels, and how have scam incidents, arrests and scam-compound rescues of Bangladeshis changed over time?

## 3. Theory
- **Lifestyle-routine activities theory (LRAT; Reyns)** explains exposure, motivated offenders and the absence of capable guardians (RQ1).
- **Scam-persuasion and influence principles** (authority, scarcity, social proof) together with **situational crime prevention** cover RQ2 and RQ4.
- **Procedural justice and police legitimacy** cover RQ3.
- **Crime script analysis** structures the coding of scam stages in Study 3.

## 4. Design: three quantitative studies (thesis by publication)

### Study 1: National online victimisation survey with two embedded experiments (RQ1–RQ3)
- **Sample:** n ≈ 1,500 adult internet users, with quotas by division, gender, age and urban/rural, recruited through a commercial online panel plus MFS-user intercepts. Results are weighted to Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) internet-user benchmarks.
- **Measures:**
  - lifetime and 12-month investment and MFS fraud victimisation
  - loss amount
  - LRAT items
  - digital literacy
  - reporting and the reasons for it
  - procedural-justice scale
- **Experiment A (vignette):** a randomised 2×2×2 design. Respondents see a mock scam message that varies authority cue, return size and payment rail, and rate their intention to invest.
- **Experiment B (reporting):** a randomised police-response vignette (respectful vs dismissive), measuring intention to report.
- **Analysis:** weighted logistic and negative-binomial regression, structural equation modelling (SEM), and experimental effects (ANOVA / regression).
- **Pre-registration:** on OSF (Open Science Framework).

### Study 2: Digital-trace analysis of scam marketing (RQ4a)
- **Data:** public Telegram channels and public Facebook pages and groups advertising "guaranteed profit" investment schemes or overseas jobs aimed at Bangladeshis. These are collected for 12 months with Telegram's public API, plus Meta Content Library access (subject to approval).
- **Measures:**
  - persuasion tactics
  - promised returns
  - payment instructions (MFS / bank / crypto)
  - job-lure destinations (Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos)
- Coding is manual (with an inter-rater reliability check) plus a Bangla text classifier.
- **Analysis:** descriptive trends, latent class analysis of scam types, and survival analysis of channel lifespan before takedown.

### Study 3: Incident and official-data time series (RQ4b)
- **Data:**
  - a systematic event dataset (2018–2027) of investment-scam incidents, mule-account arrests and scam-compound rescues of Bangladeshis, coded from Bangla and English newspapers. This builds on the Bangladesh Crime Monitor news pipeline.
  - official aggregates: BFIU suspicious-transaction reports, Bangladesh Bank MFS statistics, PCSW and police complaint counts, Cyber Tribunal caseload.
- **Optional enhancement:** anonymised police complaint records under a data-sharing agreement (as in Tuhin et al., 2025).
- **Analysis:** quantitative crime-script coding, interrupted time-series around regulatory changes (Cyber Security Act 2023/2026, MFS rules), and spatial distribution by district.

## 5. Feasibility and authenticity
- **Studies 1 and 2 depend on no gatekeeper.** The data is collected by the researcher, from public sources.
- **Study 3** uses published and public data; police records are a bonus, not a requirement.
- **No interviews with offenders and no contact with active scam networks**, which reduces both ethics and safety risk.
- **Known limitations, reported openly:**
  - online-panel selection bias, reduced by weighting and benchmark comparison
  - under-reporting in news and official data, which is treated as a measure of *recorded*, not total, crime
  - vignettes measure intentions, not behaviour
  - platform-access rules may change, with manual collection as the fallback

## 6. Ethics
- University ethics approval.
- Anonymous survey with a distress protocol and links to support services.
- Public digital data only: no account infiltration; MFS numbers, phone numbers and personal names in posts are redacted before analysis.
- Data stored securely under GDPR / university data policy.

## 7. Contribution
- **Theory:** the first large-sample test of LRAT and procedural-justice theory on fraud victimisation and reporting in Bangladesh, and experimental evidence on scam persuasion in a Global South setting.
- **Data:** the first Bangladesh fraud-victimisation estimates from a weighted sample, a public scam-marketing dataset, and a 10-year incident series (shared openly where safe).
- **Policy:** evidence for Bangladesh Bank, BFIU and MFS providers on warning design and "pinch points", and for police on reporting reform.

## 8. Fit and preparation
- **Background:** BSS in military studies (BUP), with a security and transnational-crime perspective; MSS in criminology (Dhaka University; CGPA ≈ 3.8); MSc in cyber security (UIU, completing 2027), covering payment systems, data collection and text analysis.
- **Groundwork:** a completed scoping review of cybercrime in South Asia (Bangladesh focus) in preparation for publication, and an existing news-data pipeline.

- **Year 2:** main survey and experiments; finish Study 2 collection.
