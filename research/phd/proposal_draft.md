# From mobile wallet to scam compound: the organisation and victimisation of cyber-enabled investment fraud in Bangladesh

**PhD research proposal (draft 1, about 2 pages)** · Criminology / Cybercrime · Proposed start: early 2028

## 1. Background
Cyber-enabled investment fraud (often called "pig-butchering") has become one of the world's fastest-growing criminal economies. It is run from scam compounds in Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos that hold trafficked workers and launder proceeds through mobile wallets, banks and crypto-assets (UNODC; FATF 2026).

Bangladesh sits at three points in this economy:
- Its citizens are **victims**, approached through Facebook, WhatsApp and Telegram.
- Its mobile financial services (MFS) and bank accounts act as **money-mule channels**. About BDT 75,000 crore was reportedly laundered via mobile money in 2022.
- Its young job-seekers are **trafficked into compounds** and forced to defraud others.

Yet a scoping review of Bangladeshi cybercrime research (2,207 records; 82 full-text studies charted) found that:
- Most studies are legal-doctrinal or descriptive.
- Only **9 address offending**, and **none reports interviews with offenders**.
- Only **one study** applies criminological theory to official data: a crime-script analysis of 94 police complaints by Bangladesh Police and Bangladesh Bank officers (Tuhin et al., 2025).
- Victims consistently fail to report.
- Data on MFS fraud is so scarce that researchers have used synthetic datasets.

## 2. Aim and research questions
**Aim:** to explain how investment-fraud schemes that target Bangladeshis are organised, who carries them out (including coerced workers and money mules), and how victims and institutions respond.

- **RQ1 (organisation):** What are the stages of the crime script, from first contact to cash-out, and at which "pinch points" (e.g. the move from MFS to bank transfers) can situational prevention work?
- **RQ2 (offenders and facilitators):** How are Bangladeshi money mules and scam-compound workers recruited, and how do they explain their involvement?
- **RQ3 (victims and reporting):** What predicts victims' losses and their decision to report, and how do police legitimacy and secondary victimisation shape reporting?
- **RQ4 (response):** How do police, Bangladesh Bank / BFIU and MFS providers detect, investigate and disrupt these schemes under the Cyber Security Act 2026?

## 3. Theoretical framework
**Crime script analysis** (Cornish) and **situational crime prevention** map the scheme and its points of intervention.

**Routine activity theory and lifestyle-routine activities (RAT/LRAT)** explain victim exposure.

**Neutralisation theory and social learning theory** explain how mules and recruits account for their involvement. The **victim–offender overlap** framework covers trafficked workers who are made to commit fraud.

**Procedural justice / police legitimacy** explains reporting decisions.

Integrating these approaches extends the only existing Bangladeshi theory-testing study (Tuhin et al., 2025) from the victim side to the offender and institutional sides.

## 4. Methods (sequential mixed methods, three work packages)

**WP1 – Case records (RQ1, RQ3).**
- Analyse anonymised police complaints and case files (target: more than 300 cases, 2022–2027), obtained through a data-sharing agreement with the CID Cyber Police Centre or the Police Cyber Support for Women (PCSW).
- Code script stages, payment rails, loss amounts and victim profiles.
- Analysis: descriptive and inequality measures (Gini), group comparisons, and regression on loss severity.

**WP2 – Offender and facilitator interviews (RQ2).**
- Run 30–40 semi-structured interviews with:
  - returnee scam-compound workers, recruited through IOM, migration NGOs and anti-trafficking shelters
  - convicted or diverted money mules (via lawyers and prisons)
- Analysis: reflexive thematic analysis guided by neutralisation and social learning theory.

**WP3 – Victims and institutions (RQ3, RQ4).**
- An online and in-person survey of fraud victims (n ≈ 400), measuring reporting, procedural justice and secondary victimisation.
- 20 interviews with police, BFIU / Bangladesh Bank, MFS fraud teams and Cyber Tribunal lawyers.
- An **open-source component:** a sample of scam advertisements and recruitment posts on Facebook and Telegram (job-lure and "guaranteed profit" content), coded to complement the script.

**Fallback:** if offender access (WP2) is limited, WP1 and WP3 alone still answer RQ1, RQ3 and RQ4 and produce a complete thesis.

## 5. Ethics and safety
- University ethics approval.
- Trauma-informed interviewing, with referral pathways (BRAC, IOM, survivor-support organisations).
- No identifiable case data.
- Secure storage of data.
- Researcher-safety protocols for contact with offenders.
- No covert contact with active scam networks.

## 6. Contribution
- **Theory:** the first integrated script, opportunity and neutralisation account of investment fraud from a source and victim country in the Global South.
- **Data:** the first offender-side evidence on Bangladeshi mules and coerced scam workers.
- **Policy:** evidence-based pinch points for Bangladesh Bank, BFIU and MFS providers, and victim-reporting reforms for police under the Cyber Security Act 2026.
