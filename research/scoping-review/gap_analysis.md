**Evidence base:** 109 Tier A (Bangladesh) papers read in full, of which 82 were included and charted (`charting_tierA_rows.jsonl`); plus 1,221 Tier B (rest of South Asia) abstracts coded automatically (`charting_abstracts.csv`).

## 2. Gaps

### G1. Almost no offender research
- Only 9 papers touch offending, and none uses offender interviews with reported results. R0290 claims to have interviewed scammers but reports nothing.
- Offender-side clues exist but are unstudied:
  - nationalist hacktivist groups (R0515)
  - the RAB website hacker (R0456)
  - juvenile TikTok/Likee networks (R0578)
  - scam networks and money mules (R0573)
  - Bangladeshis trafficked into scam compounds in Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos (R0179, R0573)

### G2. The theory gap
- Bangladesh studies describe; they do not explain. Of 82 papers, only R0573 tests theory with data.
- The following have never been tested on Bangladeshi data:
  - routine activity theory / lifestyle-routine activities (RAT/LRAT)
  - neutralisation
  - social learning
  - procedural justice
  - space transition theory

### G3. Missing official data and the "dark figure" of unreported crime
Reporting is consistently low, but nobody has studied *why*:
- schools: R0486
- universities: R0388
- women: R0508, R0527, R0007, R0255
- women sellers: R0422
- MFS fraud victims: R0239

Police response is described as victim-blaming (R0007, R0367). Data access is the core obstacle:
- bKash refused to share data (R0026)
- the Cyber Tribunal conviction rate is 2.86% (R0241)
- **R0573 shows police complaint data can be accessed through insider co-authors.**

### G4. MFS and investment fraud: big harm, thin evidence
- It is the fastest-growing harm. About BDT 75,000 crore was laundered via mobile money in 2022 (TIB, cited in R0573).
- But the evidence is small:
  - 12 victims (R0239)
  - one seller-side study (R0096)
  - synthetic data only (R0026)
  - 94 police complaints (R0573)
  - doctrinal pieces (R0012, R0263)
- E-commerce Ponzi fraud (Evaly and others) has only legal or descriptive coverage (R0332, R0373).

### G5. Who gets prosecuted under the cyber laws
- Case counts exist but have not been analysed criminologically:
  - 1,417 ICT Act cases, 65% under s.57 (R0436)
  - the CGS database: 1,410 DSA cases with 4,404 accused (R0248)
  - Cyber Tribunal caseload rising from 3 to 721 a year (R0578)
- Victims end up criminalised: the Nasirnagar case (R0356) and Supriti Dhar (R0367).
- The law bypasses state actors (R0529).
- The Cyber Security Act 2026 (R0008) is untested.

### G6. Gendered abuse studied outside criminology
- The strongest evidence is from linguistics (R0350, a lexicon from 2.2M comments) and HCI (R0007, R0570 on deepfakes).
- The prevalence base is national but thin: 6.6% (R0107, BAHWS) and 15.9% (R0340, icddr,b).
- Groups no one has studied: LGBTQIA+ people (R0574, one paper), rural women, and women entrepreneurs (R0422).

### G7. Evidence and courts
Digital and deepfake evidence standards are missing (R0008). Only 7 papers look at prosecution or courts, and only one of them uses justice-actor data (R0241).

## 3. PhD topic options

### Option A (recommended): investment fraud, mule networks and scam-compound trafficking affecting Bangladesh
**Working title:** *From mobile wallet to scam compound: the organisation and victimisation of cyber-enabled investment fraud in Bangladesh*

- **Questions:**
  1. How are investment scams targeting Bangladeshis organised, from the first social-media approach through MFS, bank transfers and mule accounts to cash-out?
  2. Who are the money mules and recruited or trafficked workers, and how do they explain their involvement?
  3. Why do victims report or not report, and with what outcome?
- **Theory:** crime script analysis, situational crime prevention, routine activity theory (victim side); neutralisation and social learning theory (mules and recruits). This extends R0573 to the offender side.
- **Data (mixed methods):**
  - police complaints and case files (R0573 shows the route)
  - interviews with victims, mules and returnee scam-compound workers (via IOM or migration NGOs)
  - interviews with police, Bangladesh Bank, BFIU and MFS staff
  - scam advertisements on Facebook and Telegram
- **Why it fits you:**
  - your criminology MSS
  - your cyber security MSc (payment flows, platforms)
  - your BUP / security-studies background (the transnational organised crime angle)
- **Why funders and supervisors will care:** pig-butchering and scam compounds are a top global priority (UNODC, INTERPOL, FATF 2026). The UK, Netherlands and Australia all have active online-fraud, money-mule and cyber-offender research groups.
- **Risks:**
  - access to police data, which needs a gatekeeper (consider approaching R0573's authors)
  - ethics and safety in interviewing returnees and mules
  - **Mitigation:** keep a victim-side core study that works even if offender access fails.

### Option B: the dark figure of unreported cyber victimisation and police legitimacy
**Working title:** *Why victims stay silent: reporting cyber victimisation to the police in Bangladesh*

- **Questions:**
  1. What share of victims report, and which incident and victim characteristics predict reporting?
  2. How do procedural justice, police legitimacy, gender norms and secondary victimisation shape the decision?
- **Theory:** procedural justice and legitimacy theory; RAT/LRAT for victimisation risk; a feminist lens.
- **Data:** a large survey (university, MFS-user or household sample) plus interviews with victims and police (Cyber Police Centre, PCSW).
- **Strengths:** clear theory-testing design, quantitative skills, safer ethics, and generalisable results.
- **Risk:** survey studies are common internationally, so novelty rests on the Bangladesh context and the gender angle.

### Option C: criminalisation under Bangladesh's cyber laws (ICT Act, DSA, CSA 2023, CSA 2026)
**Working title:** *Who becomes a cyber offender? Prosecution patterns under Bangladesh's cyber-security laws, 2013–2026*

- **Questions:**
  1. Who is accused, for what, by whom, and with what outcome?
  2. How do patterns change across regime changes and law reforms?
- **Theory:** labelling, selective enforcement, "lawfare" and securitisation.
- **Data:** the CGS / Ali Riaz case database, Cyber Tribunal records, news archives (the paused news pipeline fits here), and interviews with lawyers.
- **Strengths:** novel, policy-relevant, and data partly exists.
- **Risks:** politically sensitive, and better suited to law-and-society or critical-criminology supervisors than to cybercrime groups.

### Comparison

| | A: Fraud and trafficking | B: Reporting and legitimacy | C: Criminalisation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novelty | High | Medium–high | High |
| Theory testing | Yes | Yes (strongest) | Partly |
| Data feasibility | Medium (gatekeepers) | High | Medium |
| Fit with your background | Very strong | Strong | Strong |
| Funding appeal | Very high | High | Medium |
| Ethics and safety load | High | Low–medium | Medium |

**Recommendation:** Option A as the main topic, with Option B's reporting question built in as a work package. This gives both novelty and a fallback.

## 4. How our scoping review differs from R0259
R0259 is a business-oriented "PRISMA review" focused on organisations and banks. Ours is different in four ways:
1. It covers victimisation, offending and the justice system (criminological scope).
2. It includes South Asian regional comparison (Tier B).
3. It appraises the quality of the evidence (predatory outlets, inflated prevalence).
4. It produces an explicit research agenda (this document).
